# Examination systems, roles, and formats

Doctoral examination varies widely between countries and even between universities in the same
country. This file maps common patterns so the evaluation can be framed correctly. **It is not a
rulebook:** always follow the institution's regulations, forms, outcome categories, and deadlines,
and ask the user for them when they matter.

## 1. Generic outcome ladder

When the institution's categories are unknown, use this ladder and say that it is generic:

1. **Accept as submitted** — no changes required.
2. **Accept with minor corrections** — text-level changes, no new research; verified by one examiner
   or the chair.
3. **Accept with major corrections** — substantial rewriting, additional analysis, or limited
   additional experiments; verified by examiners.
4. **Revise and resubmit** — significant new work; the revised thesis is examined again, sometimes
   with a new defense.
5. **Lower award** — e.g., a master's degree, where regulations allow.
6. **Fail.**

Some systems use only pass/fail at the end of the defense, with corrections handled informally
before the final deposit; others grade the thesis.

## 2. Common systems by region

Typical patterns only; details differ by institution.

### United Kingdom and similar systems
- Usually one internal and one external examiner (two externals in some cases, e.g., when the
  candidate is a staff member).
- Examiners typically write independent preliminary reports before a private oral examination
  (viva).
- Common outcomes: award; minor corrections; major corrections; revise and resubmit; lower award
  (MPhil); fail. Correction periods vary by university.
- The viva tests the candidate's understanding and ownership of the work and can resolve concerns
  raised in the preliminary reports.

### United States and similar committee systems
- A dissertation committee chaired by the advisor, with additional faculty members and often one
  member from outside the department or institution.
- Earlier milestones (qualifying exam, proposal or prospectus) shape expectations for the final
  dissertation.
- A defense, often with a public presentation followed by closed questioning and deliberation.
- Common outcomes: pass; pass with minor or major revisions; fail. Committee members sign off on the
  final version.
- Because committee members follow the work over years, a report may focus on the defense and on
  required revisions rather than a long written assessment.

### Brazil
- A thesis committee (*banca examinadora*) whose composition — number of members and required
  external members — is set by the graduate program's regulations.
- A qualifying examination (*exame de qualificação*) usually precedes the defense.
- A public defense, after which the committee deliberates and records the result in the defense
  minutes (*ata de defesa*).
- Outcomes are typically approval or failure; many programs allow approval conditioned on
  corrections to be made before the final version is deposited. Check the program's regulations
  (*regimento*) for the exact categories and deadlines.
- Committee members often provide a written assessment (*parecer*) and detailed correction lists
  directly to the candidate.

### Nordic countries
- A preliminary assessment — by an evaluation committee or pre-examiners, depending on the country —
  decides whether the thesis may proceed to public defense.
- A public defense in which an external **opponent** leads a detailed discussion of the thesis with
  the candidate.
- A committee decides the outcome, commonly pass or fail, after the defense.
- Theses by publication (compilation theses) are common.

### Netherlands
- A doctoral committee assesses and approves the manuscript before the public defense; approval
  effectively determines that the thesis meets the standard.
- The public defense is a formal academic event with questions from committee members.

### Germany and similar systems
- Written reviews (*Gutachten*) by the reviewers, often including a grade proposal.
- An oral examination or disputation.
- Final grades are commonly expressed on the Latin scale (e.g., summa cum laude, magna cum laude,
  cum laude, rite).

### France
- External reviewers (*rapporteurs*) write reports that determine whether the defense may take place.
- A jury conducts the public defense.

### Australia and similar systems
- Typically two or more examiners, frequently external to the institution and often international.
- Examination is usually by written report, often without an oral defense.
- Categories commonly range from pass without changes, through minor and major amendments or revise
  and resubmit, to fail.
- Because there may be no defense, the written report carries the whole evaluation: questions the
  examiner would have asked orally must become explicit requests or comments.

## 3. Roles

| Role | Focus | Output adjustments |
|---|---|---|
| **External examiner** | Independent judgment against the field's standard | Full report; clear recommendation; comparison with the international state of the art |
| **Internal examiner** | Institutional standards and process; subject knowledge | Report plus attention to regulations and consistency with the external examiner |
| **Committee member** | Shared judgment, often across years | Focus on defense questions and required revisions |
| **Opponent** (Nordic defense) | Leading the public scholarly discussion | Question plan structured by chapters and contributions; brief situating presentation if required; balanced, respectful critique |
| **Chair or president** | Process, fairness, consolidation | Consolidated report; reconciled required corrections; minutes |
| **Supervisor** (pre-submission) | Readiness for examination | Mock examination; prioritized fix list; likely questions |
| **Candidate** (self-assessment) | Preparing submission or defense | Mock examination framed constructively; defense preparation |

**Conflicts of interest.** Examiners should not be co-authors, close collaborators, or have other
conflicts with the candidate or supervisor as defined by the institution. If the user mentions such a
relationship, point it out once.

**Opponent preparation.** Plan the discussion as a narrative: context and positioning of the thesis;
each contribution in order; methodology and validity; limitations; broader implications and future
work. For each block, prepare opening questions, follow-ups, and the key issue to resolve. Keep the
tone collegial — the opponent's role is to probe the work in public, not to embarrass the candidate.

## 4. Thesis by publication

Also called compilation thesis, article-based thesis, or thesis by papers. Expectations are set by
the institution (number of papers, publication status, required first authorship, format of the
introductory chapter).

**What to check:**
- **Integrating chapter** (called *kappa* in Sweden): does it present an
  overarching problem, research questions, methodology, a synthesis of the results, and a discussion
  of the overall contribution and limitations? Is it more than a summary of each paper?
- **Coherence:** do the papers address a common problem and build on each other?
- **Candidate's contribution:** is there a contribution statement for each paper (e.g., using the
  CRediT roles)? Is the candidate's role substantial in enough papers?
- **Status:** published, accepted, under review, or manuscript. Peer review is evidence of quality,
  but the examiner still assesses each paper, and unpublished manuscripts deserve closer scrutiny.
- **Currency:** related work in older papers may be outdated; the integrating chapter should update
  the positioning.
- **Consistency:** notation, terminology, and definitions across papers; contradictions between papers
  explained.
- **Errors in published papers:** if a published paper contains an error, the thesis should
  acknowledge and correct it (e.g., in the integrating chapter or an erratum note).
- **Permissions:** reuse of published material follows the publishers' policies.

## 5. Corrections and re-examination

- **Make corrections verifiable.** Number required corrections, each with location, the problem, and
  what counts as resolved. Separate required corrections from suggestions.
- **Candidate's response.** Ask for a response document mapping each required correction to the
  change and its new location.
- **Verification.** Check each correction against its resolution criterion; record resolved,
  partially resolved, or unresolved, with evidence (`report-templates.md` §7). Do not introduce new
  requirements at this stage unless the revision created new problems, and say so explicitly if it
  did.
- **Re-examination after resubmission.** Assess the revised thesis against the original report and the
  standard; acknowledge improvements; check that fixes did not introduce inconsistencies elsewhere.
